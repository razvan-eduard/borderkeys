An Android keyboard that requests zero permissions and contains no code capable of opening
a network connection. Word prediction is a deterministic engine — a compact trie plus
n-grams — written in C++ and running through JNI, not a language model.

Nothing downloads at runtime. Dictionaries and language packs are either inside the APK or
imported by you from a local file. There is no telemetry, no crash reporting, no account
and no sync. Clipboard history and the personal dictionary live in a SQLCipher-encrypted
database and are excluded from system backup and from device-to-device transfer.

* No permissions at all. Haptic feedback uses the API that needs none.
* The typing surface is a custom View drawing on a Canvas — no Compose and no XML layout
  inflation in the keyboard process.
* Several languages active at once, with no manual switching. Mixing two languages in one
  sentence is the normal case, not an exception.
* Six languages inside the app — English, Romanian, German, Spanish, French and Italian —
  and twenty-one more as packs to download from the releases page and import from a file:
  Dutch, Portuguese, Swedish, Danish, Norwegian, Finnish, Polish, Czech, Hungarian, Turkish,
  Indonesian, Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Macedonian, Greek, Armenian, Georgian,
  Hebrew and Arabic, each with the same word pairs, names and grammar the bundled six carry.
* Notices when a correction was made under the wrong language and offers the word back, or
  fixes it automatically — your choice — landing on the field's own undo history like any
  other edit.
* Swipe typing decoded geometrically on-device, with an optional radial menu: pause
  mid-swipe, without lifting, to open a ring of alternatives and slide onto one, or onto
  Cancel in the centre, all in one continuous stroke — or keep the ring open and tap. A tap
  anywhere outside it closes it and keeps the swiped word. Off by default.
* Autocorrect you can bound: off by default, and when on, the shortest word it may touch and
  how different a correction may be from what you typed are settings. A correct word the
  dictionaries do not know is left alone, a regular form of a word they do know is never
  corrected away, and backspace or a tap on the strip puts back exactly what you typed.
* Block offensive words, if you want to: one switch keeps a per-language list of profanity
  and slurs out of suggestions, corrections and learning. What you type yourself is never
  touched.
* Shift the way every keyboard does it — spent by the next letter, locked by a hold or two
  taps. Capitals off, on when the field asks, or on everywhere: one choice, since some apps
  forget to ask.
* The pressed key shows itself enlarged above your finger while it is held, and the keypress
  vibration follows the phone's own setting or one of three strengths, from its own feedback
  classes — still with no vibration permission.
* Text shortcuts: a word that stands for a longer text expands when a space or a punctuation
  mark follows it, keeps the capital you gave it, and comes back with the backspace straight
  after.
* A names dictionary built from Wikidata (CC0), so a name capitalises correctly
  mid-sentence instead of only at the start of one — while everyday words that happen to be
  somebody's name stay lower-case. One switch turns the capital off if you disagree with it.
* Thirty layouts: QWERTY, AZERTY, QWERTZ, Dvorak, Colemak, Colemak-DH, Workman, Bépo,
  ClearFlow, KasRoz and Toki Pona, the two Turkish arrangements, and the Spanish, Portuguese,
  Nordic, Danish, German, Czech and Hungarian variants with their extra letters as keys;
  Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Macedonian, Greek, Armenian and Georgian in their
  own scripts; Hebrew and Arabic read from the right, with the suggestions, the ring and the
  emoji and clipboard panels following. A number row, and a symbols page with the digits in a 3×3 block beside the
  symbols, at the right or the left, on near-square keys. Key flicks: a short drag off any key in
  one of eight directions writes a text of yours, runs a quick action or presses another key.
  Layouts of your own, written and checked in the app, drawn for any language you choose. The
  accents of any language on a letter's long press, and the keys your languages need on a free
  flick.
* An optional modifier row for terminals and editors: Esc, Tab, Ctrl, Alt and the arrows,
  with Home, End, Page up, Page down, forward Delete and Insert on offer, in the order you
  choose, above the letters or below the keyboard, each sent as the hardware key it names.
  Ctrl and Alt apply to the next key; with shift held, the arrows select. Twelve accent
  modifiers put their accent on the next letter, and a compose key spells a character from the
  keys typed after it.
* Panels that search by the word under the caret: open the emoji panel over "cake" and the
  cakes come first, over "pumpkin" the lantern, in the languages switched on; open the clipboard
  over "invoice" and the clips containing it come first.
  A long press on a clip pins, edits or deletes it.
* A personal dictionary you can see and edit: the learned words and the learned phrases each
  on a page of their own, searchable, every entry with a Delete and every word with a Block,
  and what a search finds deletable at once. The words you open sentences with are learned
  and offered first when a sentence begins. One switch gates all of it — off, the
  dictionaries alone suggest, and everything learned is forgotten after asking you — and a
  long press on any suggestion answers "Why?" with the terms that ranked it.
* A heatmap: visualise your typing accuracy and see how the keyboard adapts to your personal
  touch. Every tapped letter is read by where the tap landed, so a slip towards the next key
  is caught; with the heatmap on, the keyboard learns where your own taps land on each key and
  reads a key your way once it has enough taps. Only a summary per key is kept, never the
  taps themselves, and nothing is learned in a private field.
* Settings you can search, a tile in the quick settings, a date-and-time quick action in a
  pattern of your own, clipboard search with wildcards and regular expressions, keyboard
  opacity, and a vibration switch per kind of touch.
* Terminals typed into as terminals: in Termux and its kin the keys arrive as key events the
  moment they are pressed, the caret moves the way a terminal expects, and nothing is
  corrected or learned. A terminal app the keyboard does not recognise can be added by its
  package name.
* A row of quick actions — copy, paste, cut, select, delete a word, move the cursor, undo
  and redo, flip a word's capital, capitalise every sentence in the field — extendable with
  your own macros; above the suggestions, below the keys or down a side, in four sizes, with
  optional labels and outlines.
* Every settings card leads with what changes how typing feels; the calibration values and
  the choices made once fold under an "Advanced settings" line. A tour after setup shows
  every feature with a preview, its default and where to switch it on.
* Debug stats, one line above the settings' own typing box: the keyboard's latency, swipe
  and memory figures, rated against a baseline and shareable as text.
* Built-in themes, full manual colour control, and a library to save, import and share
  your own.
* Particle effects on the keys, the ring, the strip and the bars: an outline layer that
  traces each element's edge and a fill layer inside it, six built-in looks and your own
  saved presets. Off by default.
* Backup and restore everything this keyboard has learned and configured to a single file
  you control, with a checklist of what a file contains before any of it is applied.
  Nothing syncs anywhere on its own.
* Private mode is automatic in password fields and whenever an app sets
  IME_FLAG_NO_PERSONALIZED_LEARNING: no learning, no clipboard history, no personal
  dictionary. A Show button on the strip reveals what the field holds, and hides it again.
* Works with any installed password manager through the platform's inline autofill
  suggestions. The keyboard cannot read their contents — the platform guarantees that —
  and never sees your password.

Also included: a text assistant that runs entirely on this device. It is a draft box above
the keys — write in it, or open it with text you selected, and nothing reaches the
application until you press insert. The box edits rich text: bold, italic and lists, kept as
Markdown, and long drafts scroll inside it. With a model installed, what is in the box can be
corrected, translated, re-toned, shortened, or changed by an instruction you write
yourself, and every version is kept so you can walk back to what you started with.

This build also carries a neural swipe decoder — a small network hand-written in C++ with
no machine-learning runtime, its weights trained on a free corpus — and uses it by default:
on 500 recorded swipes it reads 91 in 100 correctly where the geometric decoder reads 77.
Layouts in other alphabets get a model of their own, trained on swipes synthesised from each
language's words. A switch turns it off, and its weights are freed the moment it goes off,
so with the switch off this build costs no more memory than the free one.

* No model is bundled and none can be downloaded automatically — there is still no
  INTERNET permission anywhere in the app. A model arrives only when you pick a GGUF file
  yourself, and it is refused unless its SHA-256 matches one of five entries compiled
  into the build.
* Inference runs in a separate process that is started when you ask for something and
  stops ninety seconds after you stop asking. The model is unloaded with it.
* Nothing is sent anywhere. The text goes to a process on this phone and the answer comes
  back to the keyboard.
* The assistant is refused entirely in password fields and wherever an application asks
  for no personalised learning.
